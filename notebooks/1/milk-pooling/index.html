Step 0: Problem statement¶

A bulk distributor supplies custom milk blends to several customers. Each customer specifies a minimum fat content, pays a fixed price per unit, and will buy no more than a stated maximum. The distributor sources raw milk from local farms, each producing milk of known fat content at a known cost.

The distributor has found cheaper raw milk at some remote farms. But it owns only one truck with a single tank for the remote route, so milk from the remote farms must be mixed in that tank before transport. That creates a pool of uniform composition, which is then blended with local milk to meet each customer’s requirement.

Three options:

  1. Business as usual --- local farms only.

  2. Buy a second truck --- keep each remote farm’s milk separate, so everything blends freely.

  3. Pool the remote farms --- one truck, one tank, one composition.

Options 1 and 2 are linear programs. Option 3 is not, and that is the point of the example.

The rest of the notebook follows the course’s five-step procedure --- mathematical model, model-size analysis, Pyomo implementation, analysis of the results --- and works all three options through each step side by side.

Note

The source data does not name the physical unit of milk. Amounts are in unnamed “units”, costs and prices are per unit, and fat content is a mass fraction. The units below are annotated as such.

The objective measures operating profit. No truck purchase price, transport cost, tank capacity, or supplier limit is included. Comparing equipment investments requires those additional data and a common time horizon.

Step 1: Mathematical model¶

It is tempting to write three models. Do not. All three options are the same model under different restrictions, and only one question changes between them:

Which farms can we buy from, and which of those must share the single tanker?

The blend quality constraint is the only one worth pausing on. It says the fat delivered to customer kkk is at least the fraction ϕkmin⁡\phi^{\min}_kϕkmin​ of the milk delivered to customer kkk, which is a linear statement even though “average fat fraction” is a ratio: multiplying through by the denominator clears it. Every term is linear, so this is a linear program.

Option 2: a second truck¶

A second truck means each remote farm’s milk travels in its own tank, so it blends freely exactly like the local milk. Nothing is pooled, so R\mathcal{R}R is still empty --- the remote farms simply move into L\mathcal{L}L:

L={Farm A,Farm B,Farm C,Farm D},R=∅.\mathcal{L} = \{\text{Farm A}, \text{Farm B}, \text{Farm C}, \text{Farm D}\}, \qquad \mathcal{R} = \emptyset .L={Farm A,Farm B,Farm C,Farm D},R=∅.
(2)

The equations are the ones written for Option 1, unchanged. It is still a linear program, and it is a relaxation of Option 3: everything Option 3 can do, Option 2 can do too, by sending the same milk down four separate tanks instead of one. So Option 2’s profit is an upper bound on Option 3’s.

Option 3: pool the remote farms¶

Only one truck is available for the remote route, so purchases from the remote farms are combined into a single pool of uniform composition, transported, and then blended with local milk. Now L\mathcal{L}L is the local farms and R\mathcal{R}R the remote ones.

This is the ppp-parameterization of the pooling problem: the pool composition is itself a decision variable, ppp.

Variables. zl,kz_{l,k}zl,k​, local milk shipped from l∈Ll \in \mathcal{L}l∈L to customer kkk; xrx_rxr​, milk bought from remote farm rrr; yky_kyk​, pooled milk delivered to customer kkk; and ppp, the fat fraction of the pool.

Every variable is nonnegative, yk≤dky_k \leq d_kyk​≤dk​, and ppp is bounded below and above by the smallest and largest fat fractions among the remote farms --- a pool cannot be richer than its richest input.

The pool fat balance can be written either as p∑r∈Rxrp \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} x_rp∑r∈R​xr​, as above and as the Pyomo code below does, or as p∑k∈Kykp \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} y_kp∑k∈K​yk​. The two agree on the feasible set, because the pool balance constraint immediately above forces ∑rxr=∑kyk\sum_{r} x_r = \sum_{k} y_k∑r​xr​=∑k​yk​.

The last two constraints contain bilinear terms: a product of the composition variable ppp with a flow variable. A bilinear term is smooth and differentiable everywhere. The feasible set of this particular model is nonconvex, as the explicit midpoint counterexample below proves. A product in a formula alone does not prove that every instance has a nonconvex feasible set or exclude all possible reformulations.

This is the model, and Options 1 and 2 are inside it. Set R=∅\mathcal{R} = \emptysetR=∅: there is no tanker, so x\mathbf{x}x, y\mathbf{y}y and ppp are never declared, the pool balance and pool fat balance disappear, the p ykp\,y_kpyk​ term in the blend quality constraint is zero, and what is left is exactly the linear program of Option 1. A nonempty remote set activates the bilinear block; the data and constraints determine its feasible geometry. The model class in the table above is a consequence of the restriction, not an independent choice.

Notation warning: ppp here is a composition, not a search direction. The symbol is the pooling literature’s own and is worth keeping, but elsewhere in this course ppp is a step.

Step 2: Model size and active constraints¶

Count declared variables, equality rows, inequality rows, and lower/upper bound sides separately. A count is an implementation check, not a feasibility proof or a formula for local dimension. For a regular equality system h(x)=0h(\mathbf x)=0h(x)=0 with full row rank mmm, the nearby equality manifold has dimension n−mn-mn−m. Dependent rows do not each remove a dimension; singular systems need separate analysis. Inequalities and bounds may be inactive, active and independent, or active and dependent.

For example, x=0x=0x=0 and 2x=02x=02x=0 are two equalities in one variable and are feasible. Two inconsistent equalities in the same variable are infeasible. More inequalities than variables is also possible: only some may bind, and even active rows can be dependent. Step 3: Pyomo implementation¶

One function, create_milk_model(local, pooled), and the two arguments are the two halves of the question from Step 1:

Optioncall
1. Business as usualcreate_milk_model(local_farms, [])
2. Buy a second truckcreate_milk_model(all_farms, [])
3. Pool the remote farmscreate_milk_model(local_farms, remote_farms)

The short add_pool_block helper is called by this builder; it is the actual pool implementation used in every solve below. There is no second model builder. Farms in local blend freely; farms in pooled share one tanker. If pooled is empty the pool variables are never declared and Pyomo is handed a linear program --- the code reduces the same way the mathematics did in Step 1.

Units. Milk here is measured in generic units, so the notebook declares a milk unit and a USD currency and hangs them on every Param and Var. Fat content stays dimensionless, because it is a mass fraction. The builder ends with assert_units_consistent, which is the part that earns the declaration: it is what would catch a price or a cost mixed into a constraint that balances amounts of milk. It cannot catch everything: fat content is dimensionless, so a fat balance and a milk balance carry the same units.

The optional fat_data argument supplies a new farm-assay dictionary for sensitivity experiments; omitting it uses the original data. The pool composition bounds are calculated from the same model parameters, so a changed assay also changes the valid mixture range. assert_units_consistent checks dimensions when called. It does not test feasibility, data validity, or optimality.

def add_pool_block(m):
    """Add the shared-composition block to the already declared farm/customer data."""
    # Amounts use the same milk unit as customer demands.
    m.x = pyo.Var(m.R, domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals, units=u.milk)
    m.y = pyo.Var(m.K, bounds=lambda b, k: (0, b.demand[k]), units=u.milk)
    m.p = pyo.Var(bounds=(min(pyo.value(m.fat[r]) for r in m.R),
                         max(pyo.value(m.fat[r]) for r in m.R)))
    @m.Constraint()
    def pool_balance(b):
        return sum(b.x[r] for r in b.R) == sum(b.y[k] for k in b.K)
    # Using sum_R x or sum_K y here agrees because of pool_balance.
    @m.Constraint()
    def pool_quality(b):
        return sum(b.fat[r] * b.x[r] for r in b.R) == b.p * sum(b.x[r] for r in b.R)
def create_milk_model(local, pooled, fat_data=None):
    """Create the milk blending/pooling model in Pyomo

    One model covers all three options. The only thing that changes is which
    farms are reachable, and which of them must share the single tanker:

        Option 1  local=local_farms, pooled=[]            business as usual
        Option 2  local=all_farms,   pooled=[]            a second truck
        Option 3  local=local_farms, pooled=remote_farms  one truck, one pool

    Farms in `local` blend freely: their milk reaches each customer as its own
    stream z_{l,k}. Farms in `pooled` share one tanker, so their milk is first
    mixed into a single stream of composition p and only then blended. If
    `pooled` is empty there is no tanker, the pool variables are never
    declared, and every remaining term is linear -- the model is an LP.

    Arguments:
        local: list of farm names whose milk blends freely
        pooled: list of farm names whose milk must share one tanker

    Returns:
        m: Pyomo concrete model
    """
    m = pyo.ConcreteModel("Milk blending and pooling")

    # Freely blended farms, L in the notes
    m.L = pyo.Set(initialize=local)

    # Pooled farms, R in the notes. Empty for Options 1 and 2.
    m.R = pyo.Set(initialize=pooled)

    # All farms sourced from, L union R
    m.S = pyo.Set(initialize=list(local) + list(pooled))

    # Customers, K in the notes
    m.K = pyo.Set(initialize=customer_names)

    # Fat fraction of the milk from farm s, phi_s [dimensionless]
    fat_values = fat if fat_data is None else fat_data
    m.fat = pyo.Param(m.S, initialize={s: fat_values[s] for s in m.S})

    # Purchase cost per unit from farm s, kappa_s [USD/milk]
    m.cost = pyo.Param(m.S, initialize={s: cost[s] for s in m.S}, units=u.USD / u.milk)

    # Price paid per unit by customer k, pi_k [USD/milk]
    m.price = pyo.Param(m.K, initialize=price, units=u.USD / u.milk)

    # Maximum demand of customer k, d_k [milk]
    m.demand = pyo.Param(m.K, initialize=demand, units=u.milk)

    # Minimum fat fraction customer k accepts, phi_min_k [dimensionless]
    m.min_fat = pyo.Param(m.K, initialize=min_fat)

    # Freely blended milk from farm l to customer k, z_{l,k} [milk]
    m.z = pyo.Var(m.L, m.K, domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals, units=u.milk)

    # Only pooled options contain the shared-composition variables and rows.
    if len(m.R) > 0:
        add_pool_block(m)

    # Helper expressions, written so the constraints below read the same way
    # whether or not a pool exists.
    def pooled_milk(b, k):
        return b.y[k] if len(b.R) > 0 else 0.0

    def pooled_fat(b, k):
        return b.p * b.y[k] if len(b.R) > 0 else 0.0

    # Maximize profit [USD]
    @m.Objective(sense=pyo.maximize)
    def profit(b):
        revenue_and_cost_local = sum(
            (b.price[k] - b.cost[l]) * b.z[l, k] for l in b.L for k in b.K
        )
        if len(b.R) == 0:
            return revenue_and_cost_local
        return (
            revenue_and_cost_local
            + sum(b.price[k] * b.y[k] for k in b.K)
            - sum(b.cost[r] * b.x[r] for r in b.R)
        )

    # Deliveries cannot exceed each customer's demand [milk]
    @m.Constraint(m.K)
    def demand_limit(b, k):
        return sum(b.z[l, k] for l in b.L) + pooled_milk(b, k) <= b.demand[k]

    # Each delivery meets its fat spec. BILINEAR when a pool exists. [milk]
    @m.Constraint(m.K)
    def blend_quality(b, k):
        fat_delivered = pooled_fat(b, k) + sum(b.fat[l] * b.z[l, k] for l in b.L)
        milk_delivered = sum(b.z[l, k] for l in b.L) + pooled_milk(b, k)
        return fat_delivered >= b.min_fat[k] * milk_delivered

    # Raises UnitsError if any constraint or the objective is inconsistent
    assert_units_consistent(m)

    return m

def model_size(m, label):
    """Count declared rows and bound sides; do not infer local dimension from counts."""
    variables = list(m.component_data_objects(pyo.Var, active=True))
    rows = list(m.component_data_objects(pyo.Constraint, active=True))
    linear = lambda row: row.body.polynomial_degree() in (0, 1)
    print(label)
    print(f"  continuous variables      {len(variables):3d}")
    print(f"  linear equality rows      {sum(c.equality and linear(c) for c in rows):3d}")
    print(f"  nonlinear equality rows   {sum(c.equality and not linear(c) for c in rows):3d}")
    print(f"  linear inequality rows    {sum(not c.equality and linear(c) for c in rows):3d}")
    print(f"  nonlinear inequality rows {sum(not c.equality and not linear(c) for c in rows):3d}")
    print(f"  lower bound sides         {sum(x.lb is not None for x in variables):3d}")
    print(f"  upper bound sides         {sum(x.ub is not None for x in variables):3d}\n")

solver = pyo.SolverFactory("ipopt")


def solve_and_report(m, title, opt=None):
    """Solve one option and print the supplier and customer tables.

    Arguments:
        m: a Pyomo concrete model from create_milk_model
        title: heading printed above the tables
        opt: solver to use; defaults to Ipopt with no options set

    Returns:
        the operating profit at the returned point
    """
    opt = solver if opt is None else opt
    results = opt.solve(m)
    assert pyo.check_optimal_termination(results), f"Solve failed for {title}"

    pooled = len(m.R) > 0

    print(f"{title}")
    if pooled:
        print(f"  pool composition p = {pyo.value(m.p):6.4f}")
    print(f"  profit             = {pyo.value(m.profit):,.2f}\n")

    # One row per farm: what its milk cost, and where it went.
    rows = []
    for s in m.S:
        row = {"fat": pyo.value(m.fat[s]), "cost": pyo.value(m.cost[s])}
        for k in m.K:
            row[k] = pyo.value(m.z[s, k]) if s in m.L else 0.0
        if pooled:
            row["Pool"] = pyo.value(m.x[s]) if s in m.R else 0.0
        rows.append(row)
    print("  Supplier report")
    display(pd.DataFrame(rows, index=list(m.S)).round(4))

    # One row per customer: what was delivered, and at what fat fraction.
    rows = []
    for k in m.K:
        row = {"min_fat": pyo.value(m.min_fat[k]), "demand": pyo.value(m.demand[k])}
        for l in m.L:
            row[l] = pyo.value(m.z[l, k])
        total = sum(pyo.value(m.z[l, k]) for l in m.L)
        fat_out = sum(pyo.value(m.z[l, k]) * pyo.value(m.fat[l]) for l in m.L)
        if pooled:
            row["Pool"] = pyo.value(m.y[k])
            total += pyo.value(m.y[k])
            fat_out += pyo.value(m.y[k]) * pyo.value(m.p)
        row["Total"] = total
        row["fat delivered"] = fat_out / total if total > 0 else np.nan
        rows.append(row)
    print("  Customer report")
    display(pd.DataFrame(rows, index=list(m.K)).round(4))

    return pyo.value(m.profit)

Why: fix ppp and look.

Fixing ppp makes every remaining term linear. The sweep solves an LP at each sampled composition. Three labeled peak candidates occur at 0.033, 0.040, and 0.045; a finite grid by itself is not a proof that it found every local or global optimum.

# Fix p and every remaining term is linear: what is left is an LP, one per
# value of p. Build the Pyomo model ONCE and re-fix p between solves --
# rebuilding it at every grid point costs far more than the solves do.
#
# The grid is split at the two customer fat requirements that fall strictly
# inside the range of p, so a sampled curve does not join across a possible switch. Each
# segment stops just short of its right-hand breakpoint, so nothing draws a
# line across a jump that is not there.

Both endpoints satisfy all bounds, balances, demand and quality requirements. Their arithmetic midpoint violates pool fat balance by 0.425 milk units of fat. Hence the original feasible set is nonconvex. This is a proof about these equations and data; it does not assert that every model with a product is nonconvex or that every conceivable reformulation is impossible.
