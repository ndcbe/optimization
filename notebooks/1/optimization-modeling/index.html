From a situation to a mathematical model¶

An optimization model specifies decision variables, a feasible set defined by equations/inequalities/domains, and one scalar objective measuring performance. For f:S⊆Rn→Rf:S\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb Rf:S⊆Rn→R, a global minimizer x∗∈S\mathbf x^*\in Sx∗∈S satisfies f(x∗)≤f(x)f(\mathbf x^*)\le f(\mathbf x)f(x∗)≤f(x) for every x∈S\mathbf x\in Sx∈S. A minimizer need not exist; the definition does not guarantee one.

Over the same feasible set,

argmin⁡⁡x∈Sf(x)=argmax⁡⁡x∈S[−f(x)].\operatorname*{arg\min}_{\mathbf x\in S}f(\mathbf x) =\operatorname*{arg\max}_{\mathbf x\in S}[-f(\mathbf x)].x∈Sargmin​f(x)=x∈Sargmax​[−f(x)].
(1)

Negation reverses the objective ordering. This is a mathematical equivalence, not a restriction that software must always minimize: Pyomo explicitly supports either objective sense.

“The process of identifying objective, variables, and constraints for a given problem is known as modeling. Construction of an appropriate model is the first step — sometimes the most important step — in the optimization process.”

Nocedal and Wright (2006), p. 2. Biegler (2010), §§1.1–1.2, pp. 1–4, motivates the same components through applications. A predictive model links adjustable quantities to outcomes; constraints encode physical laws, resource limits and admissible actions. Domain expertise and stakeholder priorities determine what belongs in those expressions. A technically accurate solve can still answer an unhelpful question if the objective or feasible alternatives were chosen poorly.

Implementation tools, including AI assistants, can help with the mechanics once a model is stated. Domain knowledge, stakeholder priorities and checking the formulation remain part of the analyst’s work.

Taxonomy of Optimization Problems¶

Reference: Chapter 1 in Biegler (2010).

The chart below organizes optimization problems by two questions: are any of the decision variables discrete, and are the objective and constraints differentiable? Those two answers largely determine which algorithms apply, which is why we start here.

Three things in this chart are worth pausing on.

Each branch ends in a chain of inclusions, read downward from the most general. On the convex side, the arrows express representability after a suitable reformulation: second-order cones admit semidefinite representations; convex QPs with affine constraints, and convex quadratically constrained QPs, admit second-order-cone epigraph formulations; and LPs are QPs with a zero quadratic term. The QP rung in this convex chain excludes nonconvex QPs. SOCP and SDP are the conic programs, and this chain is where they fit: they are the natural continuation of LP and QP, not a separate kingdom. We will not cover them, but it is worth knowing the ladder exists — a surprising number of engineering problems (robust design, some control synthesis, relaxations of nonconvex QPs) turn out to be conic once written the right way.

The mixed-integer side nests the same way. MINLP ⊇\supseteq⊇ MIQP ⊇\supseteq⊇ MILP ⊇\supseteq⊇ IP. An MIQP is just an MILP that has been allowed a quadratic objective; it earns a box because it is by far the most common MINLP you will meet in practice (portfolio selection with a cardinality limit, best-subset regression, hybrid MPC with a quadratic cost). We solve MIQPs in this course but do not study algorithms specific to them.

The bottom band is not part of the tree. This is the point students most often miss, and the reason the band is drawn as a different kind of object. Dynamic optimization, parameter estimation and experimental design, stochastic programming, logical models, decision support, and robust optimization are modeling paradigms — ways of writing a problem down. They are orthogonal to the LP/QP/NLP/MINLP axis. Concretely, each one is realized as one of the classes above:

ParadigmWhat it becomes once written down
Dynamic optimization (DAEs, collocation)the expressions determine the class: linear dynamics/costs can yield an LP, quadratic costs with linear dynamics a QP, nonlinear dynamics an NLP, and discrete choices a mixed-integer model
Parameter estimation and experimental designlinear-in-parameters least squares is a QP; nonlinear fits/design criteria often give an NLP; discrete selection can give an MILP, MIQP or MINLP
Stochastic programmingan LP, QP, NLP or mixed-integer model depending on its expressions; a finite-scenario extensive form adds scenario copies and nonanticipativity conditions
Logical models (disjunctions, GDP)reformulates to an MILP or an MINLP
Decision support (multi-objective, bilevel)can use any of these classes; a multi-objective or bilevel formulation adds further structure
Robust optimization (not covered this semester)for static linear inequalities with affine coefficient uncertainty, box/polyhedral sets can give LP counterparts and ellipsoidal sets SOCP counterparts; more general or adjustable robust models need separate analysis

Application labels do not determine the optimization class. Write the expressions and domains first, then classify the resulting finite-dimensional formulation. The robust examples above require the stated static linear structure; the uncertainty-set shape alone does not classify every robust problem. Robust optimization is a further-reading topic in this course.

The decisions here are functions, not just a vector of scalar values. Discretization produces a finite-dimensional optimization problem whose class depends on the expressions and variable domains. Detailed DAE properties and numerical methods have their teaching homes in Dynamic Optimization.

Finding a good candidate and proving global optimality are different computational tasks. Global certification can be expensive; a local solution of a nonconvex problem is not automatically a global certificate.

Responses to uncertainty¶

A deterministic optimization run treats its input data as fixed. That does not mean the inputs or the model are known accurately. In the battery example, future prices require forecasts; efficiency and usable capacity can change with age/temperature; state of charge is estimated; and omitted degradation or market rules can matter.

Decision science and the limits of a model optimum¶

In this course, decision science means studying how models, data and analytical methods support decisions under uncertainty. This is a course working definition. Optimization is one family of analytical methods; a mathematical optimum alone does not settle the broader decision process.

ViewDefinition/emphasisPeople involved
MethodsModels, data and analytical methods supporting decisions under uncertaintyAnalysts working with decision makers
ProcessProblem definition, solution design, decision making and implementation, iterated using feedbackDecision makers, stakeholders and the organization
CapacityAbility to use evidence and analysis in the intended decision processStakeholders and decision makers

Driscoll et al. (2023), pp. 148–149, describe the four-phase systems decision process as iterative and centered on decision-maker and stakeholder values. The labels are not an automatic four-step algorithm for an ill-defined situation. The older three-phase shorthand attributed to Herbert Simon, The New Science of Management Decision (1960), is intelligence, design and choice: understanding the problem, developing possible actions and choosing among them. The four-phase systems view makes implementation explicit.

Wong-Parodi et al. (2020) uses the coastal-planning example to examine how quantitative analysis supports deliberation about tradeoffs. The model, institutional process, evidence and stakeholder input work together. The closing decision-support guide gives the explicitly labelled prose paraphrase of the review’s contribution and the historical case’s limits.

Check the framing as well as the computation¶

Ackoff (1979), p. 97, criticizes assessing an optimized model through the same model assumptions: managers’ real choices and the modeled optimum can differ for reasons missing from the formulation. His statement that models simplify reality occurs in that critique, not as proof that any simplified optimum is useful. Check representation and consequences independently of the solver’s internal certificate.

The familiar paired aphorism “All models are wrong, but some are useful” is attributed to George E. P. Box. The precise printed source of that paired wording remains uncertain in the course record; do not treat the held 1976 article as verification of both halves. The methodological issue is whether a model is adequate for the decision and evidence at hand.

Rittel and Webber (1973), pp. 161–162, distinguish public-planning problems with no definitive formulation, no inherent stopping rule and solutions judged good/bad rather than simply true/false. Defining alternatives, constraints and the performance measure is part of that framing work. The claim concerns these planning contexts; it does not imply that feasibility or an optimality certificate for an already specified mathematical model is a matter of opinion. Their argument also challenges prematurely treating a wicked problem as though its goals were settled.

For a battery revenue model that ignores aging, an accurately optimized high-cycle schedule can expose a missing consequence. Discuss the operating priorities and data, then add a justified degradation cost or throughput restriction. Tightening tolerances does not add an omitted cost. A model can fail through poor numerical scaling, violated algorithm assumptions, or a mismatch between the mathematical objective and the actual decision; these are different diagnoses.

Course orientation¶

ThemeWhat the work involves
TheoryProperties of optima and algorithms; convergence hypotheses; recognizing failure conditions.
Algorithms and softwarePython/SciPy prototypes; solvers’ linear algebra and heuristics; modeling-language differentiation, model construction and debugging.
Model buildingPyomo and its extensions, with formulations informed by theory such as constraint qualifications.

Local or asymptotic convergence results require hypotheses. They do not alone establish practical performance on an arbitrary model. The final Optimization for Decision Support returns to objective construction, represented alternatives and implementation.

Part I covers continuous, integer/logical, dynamic/DAE/collocation, inference/design and stochastic modeling. Part II supplies linear algebra and real analysis, unconstrained tests and Newton/quasi-Newton methods, line-search/trust-region globalization, constrained optimality and multipliers, constraint qualifications, SQP/interior-point methods, and integer/global algorithms. The mathematical material explains modeling choices rather than replacing domain knowledge.
