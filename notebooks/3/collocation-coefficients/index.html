Notebook context¶

This notebook follows Chapter 10 of Biegler (2010). It assumes the quadrature background in Dynamic Optimization with Collocation and Pyomo.DAE and the Runge-Kutta notation in Numeric Integration for DAEs.

Three questions, answered in order:

  1. Where do the basis polynomials ℓˉj(τ)\bar\ell_j(\tau)ℓˉj​(τ) and Ωj(τ)\Omega_j(\tau)Ωj​(τ) come from?

  2. Where do the collocation points τj\tau_jτj​ come from, for each of the three families?

  3. How do those two produce the Runge-Kutta (Butcher) coefficients aj,ka_{j,k}aj,k​ and bkb_kbk​?

The two Lagrange bases¶

On finite element iii we write t=ti−1+hiτt = t_{i-1} + h_i \taut=ti−1​+hi​τ with τ∈[0,1]\tau \in [0,1]τ∈[0,1]. There are two Lagrange bases in play, and they are not the same object.

For Gauss-Legendre and right Radau, the state is interpolated through K+1K+1K+1 distinct points, including τ0=0\tau_0 = 0τ0​=0:

zK(t)=∑j=0Kℓj(τ)zij,ℓj(τ)=∏k=0k≠jKτ−τkτj−τk.\mathbf{z}^K(t) = \sum_{j=0}^K \ell_j(\tau) \mathbf{z}_{ij}, \qquad \ell_j(\tau) = \prod_{\substack{k=0 \\ k \neq j}}^K \frac{\tau - \tau_k}{\tau_j - \tau_k}.zK(t)=j=0∑K​ℓj​(τ)zij​,ℓj​(τ)=k=0k=j​∏K​τj​−τk​τ−τk​​.
(1)

The derivative is interpolated through only the KKK collocation points, and the initial-state point is not added to that list:

ℓˉj(τ)=∏k=1k≠jKτ−τkτj−τk,Ωj(τ)=∫0τℓˉj(τ′) dτ′,j=1,…,K,\bar\ell_j(\tau) = \prod_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq j}}^K \frac{\tau - \tau_k}{\tau_j - \tau_k}, \qquad \Omega_j(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} \bar\ell_j(\tau')\, d\tau', \qquad j = 1, \ldots, K,ℓˉj​(τ)=k=1k=j​∏K​τj​−τk​τ−τk​​,Ωj​(τ)=∫0τ​ℓˉj​(τ′)dτ′,j=1,…,K,
(2)

which gives the Runge-Kutta representation of the state,

zK(t)=zi−1+hi∑j=1KΩj(τ) z˙ij.\mathbf{z}^K(t) = \mathbf{z}_{i-1} + h_i \sum_{j=1}^K \Omega_j(\tau)\, \dot{\mathbf{z}}_{ij}.zK(t)=zi−1​+hi​j=1∑K​Ωj​(τ)z˙ij​.
(3)

Lobatto includes 0 among its KKK derivative nodes. Do not append another zero to form the state basis above: the denominators would vanish. The integrated derivative representation remains valid, because a degree-KKK state polynomial is determined by its initial value and its derivative at KKK distinct nodes. The derivative nodes may include zero.

Important

ℓˉj\bar\ell_jℓˉj​ under the integral, not ℓj\ell_jℓj​. The printed text of Biegler (2010) defines Ωj(τ)=∫0τℓj(τ′) dτ′\Omega_j(\tau) = \int_0^{\tau} \ell_j(\tau')\,d\tau'Ωj​(τ)=∫0τ​ℓj​(τ′)dτ′ with the (K+1)(K+1)(K+1)-point basis. That is a typo, corrected in errata2.pdf (Chapter 10, correction 1). The correct integrand is the KKK-point basis ℓˉj\bar\ell_jℓˉj​.

The tell is the degree. ℓˉj\bar\ell_jℓˉj​ has degree K−1K-1K−1, so Ωj\Omega_jΩj​ has degree KKK --- which is what the book says a few lines earlier. With ℓj\ell_jℓj​ (degree KKK) under the integral Ωj\Omega_jΩj​ would come out degree K+1K+1K+1, and the collocation equations would no longer match. We check both degrees numerically below, so the erratum is a computation here rather than a claim.

Degree and dimension. A general degree-at-most-KKK polynomial has K+1K+1K+1 coefficients. Imposing a zero constant term leaves a KKK-dimensional space. Each individual basis function is fixed once the nodes are chosen; its coefficients are not independent optimization decisions. We use degree for polynomial powers and order for convergence.

Building ℓˉj\bar\ell_jℓˉj​ and Ωj\Omega_jΩj​ symbolically¶

Both definitions are products and one integral, so sympy can carry them exactly. We keep the collocation points as exact numbers (rationals and surds) all the way through, and convert to floating point only at the very end.

def lagrange_basis(points, j):
    """Lagrange basis polynomial through `points`, equal to 1 at points[j] and 0 elsewhere.

    Arguments:
        points: list of interpolation points (sympy expressions)
        j: index of the point where this basis polynomial equals 1

    Returns:
        sympy expression in tau
    """
    expr = sym.Integer(1)
    for k, tau_k in enumerate(points):
        if k != j:
            expr *= (tau - tau_k) / (points[j] - tau_k)
    return sym.expand(expr)


def omega_basis(points):
    """Runge-Kutta basis polynomials Omega_j(tau) = int_0^tau ellbar_j(tau') dtau'.

    Arguments:
        points: the K distinct derivative nodes, including zero for Lobatto;
                do not append an extra initial-state node

    Returns:
        list of K sympy expressions in tau
    """
    return [
        sym.expand(sym.integrate(lagrange_basis(points, j), (tau, 0, tau)))
        for j in range(len(points))
    ]

Where the collocation points come from¶

The exact solution of z˙=f(z,t)\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)z˙=f(z,t) on one element is

z(ti)=z(ti−1)+∫ti−1tif(z(t),t) dt,\mathbf{z}(t_i) = \mathbf{z}(t_{i-1}) + \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i} \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z}(t), t)\, dt,z(ti​)=z(ti−1​)+∫ti−1​ti​​f(z(t),t)dt,
(4)

and collocation replaces that integral with a KKK-point quadrature rule. A rule with KKK free nodes and KKK free weights has 2K2K2K degrees of freedom, so the best possible rule is exact for polynomials of degree 2K−12K-12K−1. Theorem 10.1 of Biegler says the nodes that achieve it are the roots of a degree-KKK polynomial orthogonal on [0,1][0,1][0,1].

Constraining an endpoint leaves fewer free nodes. Derive the free nodes from the weighted orthogonality condition below, then add the fixed endpoints. Ordinary roots of a degree-KKK Jacobi polynomial do not automatically include them. Here Radau means right Radau (IIA) and Lobatto means collocation (IIIA).

For a sufficiently smooth ODE, the standard families have the following endpoint convergence orders ppp. The normalized local truncation error is O(hp)O(h^p)O(hp); the unnormalized one-step defect is O(hp+1)O(h^{p+1})O(hp+1). These statements concern the endpoint differential state, not every stage or an arbitrary DAE algebraic variable.

The free nodes are the roots of the monic polynomial Pn(τ)P_n(\tau)Pn​(τ) of degree nnn (the number of free nodes) satisfying the Gauss-Jacobi orthogonality condition

∫01w(τ) τm Pn(τ) dτ=0,m=0,…,n−1.\int_0^1 w(\tau)\, \tau^m\, P_n(\tau)\, d\tau = 0, \qquad m = 0, \ldots, n-1.∫01​w(τ)τmPn​(τ)dτ=0,m=0,…,n−1.
(5)

We build PnP_nPn​ by Gram-Schmidt on the monomials 1,τ,τ2,…1, \tau, \tau^2, \ldots1,τ,τ2,… using that weighted inner product. This is not the numerically preferred route for large KKK --- the Golub-Welsch eigenvalue algorithm is --- but it is exact, it is short, and it makes the orthogonality condition visible instead of hiding it in a library call.

The quadrature exactness degrees are respectively 2K−12K-12K−1, 2K−22K-22K−2, and 2K−32K-32K−3. Moment tests verify those degrees; they do not by themselves prove the full nonlinear ODE order. See Ascher & Petzold, Section 4.7, pp. 101–104, and Biegler, Section 10.2.2, pp. 290–293.

def monic_orthogonal(n, weight):
    """Monic degree-n polynomial orthogonal to all lower degrees on [0, 1].

    Gram-Schmidt on the monomials under the inner product
    <p, q> = int_0^1 weight(tau) p(tau) q(tau) dtau.

    Arguments:
        n: degree of the polynomial to return
        weight: sympy expression in tau, the Gauss-Jacobi weight function

    Returns:
        sympy expression in tau
    """
    basis = []
    for m in range(n + 1):
        p = tau**m
        for q in basis:
            numer = sym.integrate(weight * p * q, (tau, 0, 1))
            denom = sym.integrate(weight * q * q, (tau, 0, 1))
            p = p - (numer / denom) * q
        basis.append(sym.expand(sym.simplify(p)))
    return basis[n]


# Fixed nodes and Gauss-Jacobi weight for each family
FAMILIES = {
    "Gauss-Legendre": {"fixed": [], "weight": sym.Integer(1)},
    "Gauss-Radau": {"fixed": [sym.Integer(1)], "weight": 1 - tau},
    "Gauss-Lobatto": {
        "fixed": [sym.Integer(0), sym.Integer(1)],
        "weight": tau * (1 - tau),
    },
}


def collocation_points(family, K):
    """Collocation points tau_1 < ... < tau_K on [0, 1], derived from orthogonality.

    Arguments:
        family: one of the keys of FAMILIES
        K: number of collocation points

    Returns:
        sorted list of K exact sympy expressions
    """
    fixed = FAMILIES[family]["fixed"]
    weight = FAMILIES[family]["weight"]
    n_free = K - len(fixed)
    assert n_free >= 0, f"{family} needs K >= {len(fixed)}"

    if n_free > 0:
        P = monic_orthogonal(n_free, weight)
        free = sym.Poly(P, tau).all_roots()
    else:
        free = []

    return sorted([sym.simplify(r) for r in free] + fixed, key=float)

Building the coefficient table¶

With the points in hand, the Runge-Kutta (Butcher) coefficients are just evaluations of Ωk\Omega_kΩk​:

cj=τj,aj,k=Ωk(τj),bk=Ωk(1).c_j = \tau_j, \qquad a_{j,k} = \Omega_k(\tau_j), \qquad b_k = \Omega_k(1).cj​=τj​,aj,k​=Ωk​(τj​),bk​=Ωk​(1).
(6)

That is the entire content of the tableau. Nothing else is fitted or tuned.

def butcher_tableau(family, K, digits=None):
    """Derive the Runge-Kutta (Butcher) coefficients for a collocation family.

    Arguments:
        family: one of the keys of FAMILIES
        K: number of collocation points (stages)
        digits: if given, round the collocation points to this many digits before
            building the basis. The symbolic route is exact but the expressions
            blow up past K = 4, where the roots are no longer radicals. Pass
            digits=25 to keep the algebra fast; leave it None for exact results.

    Returns:
        c: list of K collocation points
        A: K-by-K nested list, A[j][k] = Omega_k(tau_j)
        b: list of K quadrature weights, b[k] = Omega_k(1)
        Omega: the K basis polynomials, for plotting and degree checks
    """
    c = COLLOCATION_POINTS.get((family, K)) or collocation_points(family, K)
    if digits is not None:
        c = [sym.Float(p.evalf(digits), digits) for p in c]
    Omega = omega_basis(c)

    # Deliberately no simplify() here: the entries stay exact either way, and
    # simplifying nested radicals at K > 3 is slow. Simplify at the point of use.
    A = [[Omega[k].subs(tau, c[j]) for k in range(K)] for j in range(K)]
    b = [Omega[k].subs(tau, 1) for k in range(K)]

    return c, A, b, Omega


def print_tableau(family, K):
    """Print the Butcher tableau in the usual c | A / b layout."""
    c, A, b, _ = butcher_tableau(family, K)
    print(f"{family}, K = {K}")
    for j in range(K):
        row = "  ".join(f"{float(a):>12.8f}" for a in A[j])
        print(f"  {float(c[j]):.8f} |{row}")
    print("  " + "-" * (12 + 14 * K))
    print("             |" + "  ".join(f"{float(w):>12.8f}" for w in b))

What the tableau names mean¶

Three labels have been used above without explanation: Radau IIA, Lobatto IIIA, and the LAGRANGE-RADAU string you hand to Pyomo.DAE. The Roman numerals are historical names for tableaus. They say nothing about order, and the A in IIA is not A-stability.

The family name fixes the nodes cj=τjc_j = \tau_jcj​=τj​ --- that is what the orthogonality condition above derives. The numeral fixes the A\mathbf{A}A matrix built on those nodes, and for one node set more than one A\mathbf{A}A is in circulation.

Radau. The Radau nodes can be anchored at either end of the element. Radau IA anchors τ1=0\tau_1 = 0τ1​=0; Radau IIA anchors τK=1\tau_K = 1τK​=1. FAMILIES["Gauss-Radau"] above fixes the node at 1, so everything this notebook prints for Gauss-Radau is Radau IIA --- the same choice scheme="LAGRANGE-RADAU" makes. Radau IIA has a nonsingular A\mathbf AA and is stiffly accurate: its last row is bT\mathbf b^TbT. Together these give stiff decay through R(∞)=1−bTA−11=0R(\infty)=1-\mathbf b^T\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf 1=0R(∞)=1−bTA−11=0. An endpoint node alone is insufficient: Lobatto IIIA also includes 1.

Which Lobatto is algebraically stable?¶

The distinction decides one row of the comparison table in the collocation lecture, so it is worth pinning down. A Runge-Kutta method is algebraically stable when b≥0\mathbf{b} \geq \mathbf{0}b≥0 componentwise and the K×KK \times KK×K matrix

Mjk=bjajk+bkakj−bjbkM_{jk} = b_j a_{jk} + b_k a_{kj} - b_j b_kMjk​=bj​ajk​+bk​akj​−bj​bk​
(7)

is positive semidefinite (Ascher & Petzold, Exercise 4.13, p. 119; Burrage & Butcher, Theorem 2.1). Algebraic stability implies AN-stability (Burrage & Butcher, Theorem 3.3). Their converse applies when the abscissas cjc_jcj​ are distinct, as in these families. Keep that qualification when comparing the two definitions; the numerical-integration guide gives the contractivity definition.

Burrage & Butcher (1979) list the algebraically stable classes on printed p. 51: the Gauss methods, Radau IA and Radau IIA, and “the Lobatto IIIC methods”. IIIC is on that list; IIIA is not. Ascher & Petzold Exercise 4.13(c) asks for the other half: show that the trapezoidal method, and hence Lobatto collocation, is not algebraically stable.

The reason is one line of arithmetic, and it holds at every KKK. The Lobatto node set starts at τ1=0\tau_1 = 0τ1​=0, and Ωk(0)=0\Omega_k(0) = 0Ωk​(0)=0 by construction, so the first row of the collocation A\mathbf{A}A vanishes. With a11=0a_{11} = 0a11​=0,

M11=2b1a11−b12=−b12<0,M_{11} = 2 b_1 a_{11} - b_1^2 = -b_1^2 < 0,M11​=2b1​a11​−b12​=−b12​<0,
(8)

and a symmetric matrix with a negative diagonal entry is not positive semidefinite. Both halves are checked below.

def stability_matrix(A, b):
    """Algebraic-stability matrix M_{j,k} = b_j a_{j,k} + b_k a_{k,j} - b_j b_k.

    Arguments:
        A: K-by-K nested list of Butcher coefficients
        b: list of K quadrature weights

    Returns:
        sympy Matrix, K by K
    """
    K = len(b)
    return sym.Matrix(
        K, K, lambda j, k: sym.simplify(b[j] * A[j][k] + b[k] * A[k][j] - b[j] * b[k])
    )

Important

Same nodes, different methods. Collocation on the Lobatto nodes gives IIIA. The negative diagonal entry of M\mathbf MM proves that it is not algebraically stable (and, with distinct nodes, not AN-stable). IIIC uses different coefficients and is algebraically stable.

For two stages, IIIA is the trapezoidal method, R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1−z/2)R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1-z/2)R(z)=(1+z/2)/(1−z/2), whose limit is -1. Stiff accuracy, stiff decay, and algebraic stability are different tests. A singular A\mathbf AA prevents using 1−bTA−111-\mathbf b^T\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf 11−bTA−11; it does not make R(z)R(z)R(z) or its limit undefined.

The Lobatto comparison in the handout means IIIA unless it explicitly names IIIC. Pyomo.DAE’s Lagrange schemes do not produce IIIC.

Measure quadrature exactness¶

Apply ∑kbkckd\sum_k b_k c_k^d∑k​bk​ckd​ to monomials and compare with ∫01τd dτ=1/(d+1)\int_0^1\tau^d\,d\tau=1/(d+1)∫01​τddτ=1/(d+1). This measures quadrature degree of exactness. For a nonlinear ODE, the stage values are also approximations; quadrature moments alone are not all Runge-Kutta order conditions.

def highest_exact_degree(family, K, max_degree=14):
    """Highest polynomial degree the K-point quadrature rule integrates exactly on [0, 1]."""
    c, _, b, _ = butcher_tableau(family, K, digits=25)
    c_float = np.array([float(p) for p in c])
    b_float = np.array([float(w) for w in b])
    for d in range(max_degree + 1):
        rule = float(np.dot(b_float, c_float**d))
        exact = 1.0 / (d + 1)
        if not np.isclose(rule, exact, rtol=0, atol=1e-12):
            return d - 1
    return max_degree

Using the coefficients¶

The free nodes come from a weighted orthogonal polynomial; add the fixed endpoints afterward. Integrating the derivative basis gives ajk=Ωk(cj)a_{jk}=\Omega_k(c_j)ajk​=Ωk​(cj​) and bk=Ωk(1)b_k=\Omega_k(1)bk​=Ωk​(1). Verify partition of unity, row sums, weights, and the actual stability function.

The Roman numeral identifies a tableau, not only a node set. Collocation gives Lobatto IIIA; IIIC uses different coefficients. For the distinctions among algebraic stability, AN-stability and stiff decay, see Numeric Integration for DAEs.
