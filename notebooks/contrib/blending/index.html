Original Linear Blending Problem¶

Reference: https://jckantor.github.io/ND-Pyomo-Cookbook/notebooks/02.03-Linear-Blending-Problem.html

A brewery receives an order for 100 gallons of 4% ABV (alcohol by volume) beer. The brewery has on hand beer A that is 4.5% ABV that cost USD 0.32 per gallon to make, and beer B that is 3.7% ABV and cost USD 0.25 per gallon. Water could also be used as a blending agent at a cost of USD 0.05 per gallon. Find the minimum cost blend that meets the customer requirements.

How this project extends the original¶

  • +/-10% of uncertainty for the product demands with equal probabilities

  • +/-20% of uncertainty for the raw materials’ costs

  • Setting minimum required water usage for blending = at least 10 gallons

Note

What the models below do and do not include. Only the cost uncertainty enters the optimization problem. The demand is held at its mean value of 100 gallons in the volume constraint, so the spread in the cost histogram further down comes entirely from the three ingredient costs. Demand samples are drawn, but they are deliberately never used.

Two smaller mismatches with the list above are worth naming while reading the code. Both uncertainties are sampled from normal distributions whose standard deviation is the stated percentage of the mean, not from two equally likely scenarios. And the +/-10% is therefore one standard deviation, not a hard range.

Letting the demand uncertainty change the blend requires a two-stage model: the blend is chosen before the demand is known, and a second, scenario-dependent decision covers whatever shortfall or surplus appears afterwards. That is the subject of stochastic programming, Stochastic Programming, so we leave it here as an extension rather than build it now.

Before we start to consider uncertainties, let’s solve the problem including only the minimum water constraint.

Objective Function¶

The objective is to minimize the expected cost of production. The cost is influenced by uncertainty in the cost of ingredients and is expressed as follows:

Minimize Cost=(CAxA+CBxB+CWxW)\text{Minimize } Cost = \left( C_{A} x_A + C_{B} x_B + C_{W} x_W \right)Minimize Cost=(CA​xA​+CB​xB​+CW​xW​)
(1)

Where:

  • Cost{Cost}Cost is the cost of blending.

  • CA,CB,CWC_{A}, C_{B}, C_{W}CA​,CB​,CW​ are the costs of Beer A, Beer B, and Water.

  • xA,xB,xWx_A, x_B, x_WxA​,xB​,xW​ are the decision variables representing the volumes of Beer A, Beer B, and Water respectively.

Constraints¶

  1. Volume Constraint: The total volume of Beer A, Beer B, and Water must equal the mean demand:

    xA+xB+xW=Dx_A + x_B + x_W = DxA​+xB​+xW​=D
    (2)

    Where DDD is the mean demand.

  2. ABV Constraint: The final blend must have an alcohol content of 4% ABV. This constraint is expressed as:

    ABVtarget=ABVAxA+ABVBxB+ABVWxWxA+xB+xWABV_{target} = \frac{ABV_A x_A + ABV_B x_B + ABV_W x_W}{x_A + x_B + x_W}ABVtarget​=xA​+xB​+xW​ABVA​xA​+ABVB​xB​+ABVW​xW​​
    (3)

    Where:

    • ABVtargetABV_{target}ABVtarget​ is the target ABV (4%).

    • ABVA,ABVB,ABVWABV_A, ABV_B, ABV_WABVA​,ABVB​,ABVW​ are the ABV values for Beer A, Beer B, and Water respectively.

  3. Minimum Water Constraint: There must be at least 10 gallons of Water in the blend:

    xW≥10x_W \geq 10xW​≥10
    (4)

Implementation without uncertainty¶

def beer_blend(vol, abv, data):
    C = data.keys()
    model = pyo.ConcreteModel()
    # Variable
    model.x = pyo.Var(C, domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals)

    # Objective
    model.cost = pyo.Objective(expr=sum(model.x[c] * data[c]["cost"] for c in C))

    # Constraints
    model.vol = pyo.Constraint(
        expr=vol == sum(model.x[c] for c in C)
    )  # The final demand volume constraint
    model.abv = pyo.Constraint(
        expr=0 == sum(model.x[c] * (data[c]["abv"] - abv) for c in C)
    )  # The final abv of blending
    model.min_water = pyo.Constraint(expr=model.x["W"] >= 10)  # Minimum water volume

    # Solve Problem
    solver = pyo.SolverFactory("appsi_highs")
    results = solver.solve(model)
    assert pyo.check_optimal_termination(results), (
        f"Solve failed: status={results.solver.status}, "
        f"termination={results.solver.termination_condition}"
    )

    # Round for display: the solver returns floats that can sit a few ULPs off
    # a round number (e.g. 83.75000000000003), which is noise, not information.
    print("Optimal Blend")
    for c in data.keys():
        print(f"   {c} : {model.x[c]():.4g} gallons")
    print()
    print(f"Volume = {model.vol():.6g} gallons")
    print(f"Cost = ${model.cost():.4f}")

Monte Carlo Simulation¶

The Monte Carlo simulation is used to evaluate the cost under different scenarios of ingredient cost uncertainty. By generating random values for the costs based on given distributions, we can calculate the expected value and standard deviation of the total cost. The demand stays at its mean value of 100 gallons throughout, as explained in the note above.

  • Expected Cost: The average cost across all simulated scenarios.

  • Standard Deviation of Cost: A measure of the variability in cost due to uncertainty in the ingredient prices.

The results are visualized to understand the distribution of the cost and the optimal blend composition.

Mathematical Formulation of the Monte Carlo Blending Problem¶

The blending problem aims to determine the optimal volumes of Beer A, Beer B, and Water required to produce a target volume of beer with a specific Alcohol By Volume (ABV) while minimizing the cost.

Notice which symbols carry the scenario index iii: the costs CA,i,CB,i,CW,iC_{A,i}, C_{B,i}, C_{W,i}CA,i​,CB,i​,CW,i​ do, and nothing in the constraints does. That is the whole of what “under uncertainty” means in this notebook.

Objective Function¶

The objective is to minimize the expected cost of production. The cost is influenced by uncertainty in the cost of ingredients and is expressed as follows:

Minimize E[Cost]=1N∑i=1N(CA,ixA+CB,ixB+CW,ixW)\text{Minimize } E[\text{Cost}] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( C_{A,i} x_A + C_{B,i} x_B + C_{W,i} x_W \right)Minimize E[Cost]=N1​i=1∑N​(CA,i​xA​+CB,i​xB​+CW,i​xW​)
(5)

Where:

  • E[Cost]E[\text{Cost}]E[Cost] is the expected cost.

  • NNN is the number of scenarios.

  • CA,i,CB,i,CW,iC_{A,i}, C_{B,i}, C_{W,i}CA,i​,CB,i​,CW,i​ are the costs of Beer A, Beer B, and Water in scenario iii.

  • xA,xB,xWx_A, x_B, x_WxA​,xB​,xW​ are the decision variables representing the volumes of Beer A, Beer B, and Water respectively.

Constraints¶

  1. Volume Constraint: The total volume of Beer A, Beer B, and Water must be at least equal to the mean demand:

    xA+xB+xW≥Dx_A + x_B + x_W \geq DxA​+xB​+xW​≥D
    (6)

    Where DDD is the mean demand. DDD is one fixed number, the same in every scenario; the sampled demands never appear here.

  2. ABV Constraint: The final blend must have an alcohol content of 4% ABV. This constraint is expressed as:

    ABVtarget=ABVAxA+ABVBxB+ABVWxWxA+xB+xWABV_{target} = \frac{ABV_A x_A + ABV_B x_B + ABV_W x_W}{x_A + x_B + x_W}ABVtarget​=xA​+xB​+xW​ABVA​xA​+ABVB​xB​+ABVW​xW​​
    (7)

    Where:

    • ABVtargetABV_{target}ABVtarget​ is the target ABV (4%).

    • ABVA,ABVB,ABVWABV_A, ABV_B, ABV_WABVA​,ABVB​,ABVW​ are the ABV values for Beer A, Beer B, and Water respectively.

  3. Minimum Water Constraint: There must be at least 10 gallons of Water in the blend:

    xW≥10x_W \geq 10xW​≥10
    (8)

Create the Pyomo Monte Carlo model¶

# creating a Pyomo model
model = pyo.ConcreteModel()

# generating continuous random demands and costs for Monte Carlo simulation
num_scenarios = 10000000
# One shared Generator, so the four streams below are independent of each other
# (reusing the same integer seed for each call would make them identical draws).
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# The demand samples are deliberately never used again: the volume constraint below
# holds the demand at mean_demand, so only the cost samples move the results. The draw
# is kept for the two-stage stochastic programming version of this example, and because
# removing it would shift the shared random stream and change every number below.
demands = norm.rvs(
    loc=mean_demand, scale=std_dev_demand, size=num_scenarios, random_state=rng
)
costs_A = norm.rvs(
    loc=mean_cost_A, scale=std_dev_cost_A, size=num_scenarios, random_state=rng
)
costs_B = norm.rvs(
    loc=mean_cost_B, scale=std_dev_cost_B, size=num_scenarios, random_state=rng
)
costs_W = norm.rvs(
    loc=mean_cost_W, scale=std_dev_cost_W, size=num_scenarios, random_state=rng
)

# decision variables (note: decisions must be the same across scenarios)
model.x_A = pyo.Var(domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals)
model.x_B = pyo.Var(domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals)
model.x_W = pyo.Var(domain=pyo.NonNegativeReals)


# objective function: minimize the expected cost
def Objective_rule(model):
    return sum(
        (costs_A[i] * model.x_A + costs_B[i] * model.x_B + costs_W[i] * model.x_W)
        / num_scenarios
        for i in range(num_scenarios)
    )


model.Obj = pyo.Objective(rule=Objective_rule, sense=pyo.minimize)


# constraints
# volume constraint: the demand is fixed at mean_demand, NOT sampled from demands
def Volume_rule(model):
    return model.x_A + model.x_B + model.x_W >= mean_demand


model.Volume = pyo.Constraint(rule=Volume_rule)


# ABV constraint
def Abv_rule(model):
    return abv_target == (abv_A * model.x_A + abv_B * model.x_B + abv_W * model.x_W) / (
        model.x_A + model.x_B + model.x_W
    )


model.Abv = pyo.Constraint(rule=Abv_rule)


# minimum water constraint
def Min_Water_rule(model):
    return model.x_W >= 10


model.Min_Water = pyo.Constraint(rule=Min_Water_rule)
# solving the problem
solver = pyo.SolverFactory("ipopt")
results = solver.solve(model)
assert pyo.check_optimal_termination(results), (
    f"Solve failed: status={results.solver.status}, "
    f"termination={results.solver.termination_condition}"
)

# extracting results
x_A_value = pyo.value(model.x_A)
x_B_value = pyo.value(model.x_B)
x_W_value = pyo.value(model.x_W)

# presenting optimal values for volume of ingredients
print(f"Optimal Volume of Beer A: {x_A_value:.2f} gallons")
print(f"Optimal Volume of Beer B: {x_B_value:.2f} gallons")
print(f"Optimal Volume of Water: {x_W_value:.2f} gallons")

# running Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate the cost under different scenarios
total_costs = []
for i in range(num_scenarios):
    cost = costs_A[i] * x_A_value + costs_B[i] * x_B_value + costs_W[i] * x_W_value
    total_costs.append(cost)

# calculating and printing expected cost and standard deviation
expected_cost = np.mean(total_costs)
std_dev_cost = np.std(total_costs)
print(f"Expected Total Cost: {expected_cost:.2f} USD")
print(f"Standard Deviation of Total Cost: {std_dev_cost:.2f} USD")

Degrees of Freedom Analysis¶

  • Decision Variables:

    Volume of Beer A, Beer B, and Water (3 Variables)

  • Constraints:

    Volume constraint, ABV constraint, and Minimum water constraint (3 constraint)

  • Degree of Freedom (DoF):

Degree of Freedom=Number of Variables−Number of Constraints\text{Degree of Freedom} = \text{Number of Variables} - \text{Number of Constraints}Degree of Freedom=Number of Variables−Number of Constraints
(9)
3−3=0 DoF3 - 3 = 0 \text{ DoF}3−3=0 DoF
(10)

This model has zero degree of freedom. The three constraints pin down a single feasible blend, so the cost uncertainty has nothing to choose between: sampling the costs changes the value of the objective but not the point that minimizes it, which is why the two solutions above agree exactly. Zero degrees of freedom is also the reason this model cannot answer a demand other than 100 gallons. Giving it that freedom is the two-stage formulation described in the note at the top, and is the natural place to pick this example up again in Stochastic Programming.
